AI Juggler · 0.5 · the Floor · research

An office you look into from above, at an angle

The maintainer asked for a Floor that looks like a real office, seen from above at an angle with perspective, not a bird's-eye plan. Windows and doors sit on the back walls, and everything is generated. This page covers how games with that camera do it, what it means for AI Juggler, and three look directions drawn by our own code from the same sample office.

Recommendation

Go with B: real 3D in the webview, built with three.js, from a fixed angled camera with perspective.

  • Back walls: windows and doors stay up; the walls facing you are cut down.
  • Camera: turns in four 90° steps, as Two Point Campus does, and zooms in three levels (Building, Floor, Desk).
  • Building level: uses C's stacked-floor cutaway to show every floor and who needs you on each.
  • Rendering: lazy-loaded, drawn only when something changes, and at a lower internal resolution while people animate.
  • Fallback: the top-down 2D renderer (already prototyped) on machines without hardware WebGL, which matters most for Linux.

Why not A: isometric is a parallel projection, so it has no perspective, which is what was asked for. It is the cheaper fallback look if B's measured cost misses the budget. Nothing is built yet; the next step is your pick, then a measured B prototype.

Procedural building references

  • Townscaper picks modules with Wave Function Collapse on an irregular grid, stencils windows in after placement, and decorates last. Restraint keeps it readable when zoomed out.
  • Tiny Glade gives each building element its own generator, and walls re-arrange live (Rust, on a modified Bevy).
  • Wave Function Collapse (mxgmn) and Marian Kleineberg's WFC city use modules with socket faces, which suits walls, windows and doors.
  • A small office-plan generator (ThreeMojo; weak source) uses a corridor with bands of rooms and a core, with desks preferring windows and room left for doors to swing.

Techniques worth borrowing

  1. Cutaway by default: outer walls stay up and the walls between you and the room drop (The Sims).
  2. One floor at a time, with a floor stepper that hides the floors above (The Sims).
  3. A stacked side-cutaway to see every floor at once (Project Highrise, Tiny Tower, Fallout Shelter): our Building level.
  4. Overlay views that tint everything by one question, such as "who needs me?" or "who's near a limit?" (Two Point data views).
  5. Bubbles that name the thing wanted, not a bare icon (Tiny Tower).
  6. Simple, stylised shapes that stay readable small (Two Point's claymation, Townscaper's restraint).
  7. Structure first, decoration second, with windows cut after placement, so clutter never breaks the layout (Townscaper).
  8. A fixed angle with stepped rotation gives most of the 3D payoff while keeping walls and occlusion simple.

Camera options

  • 2.5D isometric sprites (Canvas 2D or PixiJS):
    • Cheapest, and closest to what we already have.
    • Sprites must be drawn per facing direction.
    • No real perspective.
  • 3D in the webview (three.js on WebGL):
    • Real perspective, lighting and shadows.
    • Procedural geometry is trivial: rooms are boxes.
    • About 170 KB gzipped, loaded only with the page.
    • Works in WKWebView and WebView2.
    • WebKitGTK can silently run WebGL in software, and Tauri's own docs advise a non-WebGL fallback.
  • Native (wgpu or Bevy): ruled out in the first round. Embedding a native view in the Tauri window is fragile, doesn't work on Wayland, and adds 30–50 MB.

Generating the building

  • Floor plan: the stable bay grid from the first prototype carries over unchanged. A session keeps its desk, an App grows by annex bays, and a full floor opens the next.
  • Shell:
    • A rectangle with a core (elevator, stairs) on one wall.
    • Reserved rooms against the back and side walls: meeting rooms for pools, the server room for This Mac, a kitchen, an archive and your office.
  • Walls:
    • Windows are placed on a rhythm, skipping wherever a room, board or door sits.
    • Doors go where rooms meet the open floor and on the core.
    • Whiteboards, corkboards and clocks fill the gaps between windows.
  • Outside: a seeded skyline outside the windows, which follows day and evening with the theme.
  • People: built from parts once and cached, as before, now as a few low-poly boxes and spheres posed rigidly (typing, waving, asleep). No skeletal animation is needed.

Zoom levels in B

  • Building: C's cutaway with every floor stacked, each showing a needs-you count and its Apps. Click a floor to go in.
  • Floor: the angled view of one floor, with zone labels, badges over desks and fixed-size bubbles. Turn in four steps; the walls facing you drop automatically.
  • Desk: the camera dollies to a desk. The monitor's tasks and subagents show as a crisp HTML card beside it.
  • Who needs you: a toggle in the style of Two Point's data views. It tints every desk by state and dims the rest.

Cost: what B must do to stay in budget

  • At rest: 0 frames. Render only on change (three.js's render-on-demand pattern), and never while hidden, scrolled away, or with Motion off.
  • Animating: people move at about 8 fps, rendered at a lower internal resolution (pixel ratio 1) and sharpened to full resolution once things settle. The first prototype showed the cost is pixels per frame, not scene size.
  • Shadows are drawn once and refreshed only when furniture changes (shadowMap.autoUpdate = false).
  • InstancedMesh for desks, chairs, monitors and plants: about 570 draw calls drop to roughly 30.
  • Measured here: a full frame of mock B takes about 2 ms on the CPU with 570 meshes and no instancing (browser pane, Apple silicon). GPU time and CPU while animating are not measured yet; that is the first thing the B prototype measures.
  • Budget, unchanged: zero at rest, under 3 percentage points of a core while animating, proved in the real app (the Floor's budget task).

Accessibility

  • The list view, keyboard paths, Needs-you list and polite announcements from the first round all carry over unchanged.
  • Reduced motion means no camera flights (cuts instead), no idle animation and still poses.
  • Rotation comes in steps, never free spinning, which is kinder to vestibular sensitivity.
  • Never colour alone: every state is a pose, a glyph and a word.
  • Red alert stays a steady tint with no flashing (WCAG 2.3.1).
  • The canvas is a picture, with the same facts in HTML beside it.

What carries over from the first round

  • The data map: which state, prop or room means what.
  • The stable layout and multi-floor rules.
  • People from parts, cached.
  • Re-create in Settings.
  • The list view.
  • The cost lessons: draw on demand, and pay for pixels, not objects.
  • The measuring script, and the 2D renderer as the Linux fallback.

Only the camera and the drawing change.
