AI Juggler · 0.5 · the Floor · the picked look

A fixed-angle office in fine pixel art

The maintainer picked a look between B and C:

  • A's high, angled camera and its see-through cutaway;
  • pixel art, but at a fine pixel size rather than chunky low-res pixels;
  • no rotating 3D: fixed angle, pan and zoom only.
It is possible, and the mock below is that look, drawn by our own code.

Yes. Here's how.

  • The office is still built as a simple 3D scene of boxes and spheres, generated from the data as before.
  • It is seen from one fixed camera, high up, looking down at about 38° with real perspective. You can pan and zoom, but never rotate.
  • The scene is drawn at a reduced internal resolution: 1 art pixel = 2–4 screen pixels. It is scaled up with hard edges, then passed through a small pixel-art filter that limits the colours and outlines every object.
  • Labels and bubbles stay crisp on a separate text layer in a pixel font.

Because it renders a quarter to a sixteenth of the pixels, it costs less than the smooth 3D look, not more. It still draws nothing at rest. The other way, hand-drawn angled pixel sprites, is compared below; it loses the perspective and costs far more art.

02 · cost

What a frame costs, by pixel size

Each frame is timed with the GPU made to finish, so it is the full cost of one redraw. The table holds this mock's numbers measured in the in-app browser on an Apple-silicon Mac. The headline: about 2 ms per frame at any pixel size, so animating at 8 fps costs under 2% of one core before any optimisation.

At rest and while animating

  • At rest: no frames, so no cost. The scene redraws only when data changes, you pan or zoom, or someone on screen is animating.
  • Animating (typing, waving, bots, a walker): about 8 frames a second at the chosen pixel size. That is how pixel art animates anyway, and it keeps the cost a small fraction of a core.
  • Never while the page is hidden or scrolled away, at Building zoom, or with Motion off.
  • Shadows are drawn once and reused; furniture only moves when the layout changes.
  • Repeated things (desks, chairs, monitors, plants) become instanced meshes in the build. The mock has about 560 separate objects; instancing brings that to a few dozen draw calls.

Making pixel art behave in 3D

  • No shimmer when panning: the camera moves in whole art pixels. The remainder is a smooth screen offset, the same trick the 2D prototype used.
  • Zoom in steps (Floor, Closer, Desk), so the art pixel size stays whole and crisp.
  • One fixed angle, so walls, cutaways and glass are designed once. Nothing turns, which is what was asked for.
  • Outlines come from depth edges, and colour steps from a small quantiser. Both are tunable, so the look can sit anywhere between "clean pixel" and "retro".
  • Text is never pixelated: labels and bubbles are a crisp overlay that redraws only when they change.

A third way, rendering the 3D scene into sprite sheets once at start-up and then composing them in 2D, keeps the 3D look and lowers the cost further. But it loses perspective (each sprite has its own viewpoint) and adds a baking step. It's worth keeping in reserve only if way 1 misses the budget.

04 · carries over

What stays from the earlier rounds

Data and layout

The stable bays, annexes and floors; the map of rooms (meeting = pool, server = This Mac, kitchen, archive, your office, lobby); people built once from parts and cached; Re-create in Settings.

States and props

The pose for each state; bubbles that name what is wanted; decay into dust and trash; desk fire; red alert as a steady tint; whiteboard, corkboard and clocks.

Floors

One floor in this angled view, plus a Building level that stacks the floors as cutaways (C), with the needs-you count on each. Floors are switched by the elevator or by tabs.

Accessibility

The list view, keyboard paths, Needs-you list and polite announcements. Reduced motion means no animation and instant camera moves. Every state is a pose, a glyph and a word, never colour alone.
